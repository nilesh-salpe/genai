What are we actually building?

Before a single formula, this page answers one question in plain language: what is a neural network, what does "training" mean, and what does a Transformer add that makes it able to write? No equations. No code. Just the shape of the idea — so that when the maths arrives in Part 1, you already know what it's *for*.

01The machine

A neural network is a box that guesses, and gets corrected

Picture a box with a row of dials on the front, an input slot, and an output slot. Drop a number in, and depending on where the dials are set, a number comes out. That's the whole machine. Nothing inside it is smart — it's just dials turning inputs into outputs.

The trick is not the box. The trick is what happens when the box is wrong. Show it an input where you already know the right answer, compare its guess to that answer, and nudge every dial a tiny bit in the direction that would have made the guess less wrong. Do that millions of times, on millions of examples, and the dials settle into positions where the box is usually right — not because anyone told it the rule, but because it was nudged toward "less wrong" so many times that it found one.

02Scaling it up

A language model is the same box, guessing the next word

Swap the input: instead of one number, feed in a stretch of text. Swap the output: instead of one number, ask for a guess at whatever word comes next. Everything else is identical — guess, compare to the word that actually came next, nudge the dials toward "less wrong." Do this across most of the readable internet, with a box that has billions of dials instead of a handful, and the box gets remarkably good at guessing the next word.

That's a large language model. Not a plan, not a lookup table of facts, not a mind that decided what to say — a very large version of the same guess-compare-nudge machine from above, trained on one relentless task: predict what comes next. Everything that looks like reasoning or knowledge is a side effect of getting extremely good at that one task, at a scale large enough for patterns of grammar, fact, and argument to become the most efficient way to keep guessing correctly.

Your phone's keyboard already does a tiny version of this every time it suggests the next word. An LLM is that same instinct, scaled up by roughly a billion.

03The missing piece

Why "guess the next word" isn't enough on its own

Guessing the next word requires using everything said so far — but not all of it equally. In "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too small," the word it only makes sense once you've weighed trophy against suitcase and decided which one "small" plausibly describes. A model that reads left to right and treats every earlier word the same has no way to do that weighing.

The fix — the specific idea a Transformer adds — is to let every word look at every other word in the sentence and decide, for itself, how much each one matters right now. Try it below: click a word and watch which other words it leans on.

04Why maths, then

Intuition tells you what happens. Maths lets you build it.

Everything above is true, and everything above is also something you could only ever watch happen to someone else's model. "Nudge the dials toward less wrong" isn't a recipe until you can say exactly which direction is "less wrong" for each of billions of dials, computed in a way a computer can actually carry out — and that requires the specific tools the rest of this course builds: how to measure "wrong" as a single number, how to find the direction that shrinks it fastest, and how to turn "look at every other word" into an actual computation instead of a hand-drawn picture.

None of it is exotic. Every piece is built from arithmetic you already have, one small idea at a time, each one checked against numbers you can verify by hand before it's trusted. That's the whole method of this course: intuition, then the maths that makes it real, then the numbers that prove it.
