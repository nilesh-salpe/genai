One block wasn't the whole paper

Part 1 built one flavor of transformer block — the causal, decoder-only kind every current LLM uses. "Attention Is All You Need" actually proposed something larger: two stacks talking to each other, because translation needs a fundamentally different shape than next-word prediction. This part fills in what Part 1 left out — encoder vs. decoder, the wire between them, why the paper mattered, and how a trained model finally turns numbers back into words.

01Why one block wasn't enough

Reusing Part 1's own sentence, one more time

Part 1 §11 used "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired" to motivate attention: to know what "it" refers to, a model needs "tired" — six words later. That's fine for a block that gets the whole sentence at once. It's a real problem for a decoder, which by design — the causal mask from Part 1 §11 — is never allowed to look forward.

Predict first — could a decoder alone translate this sentence?

Imagine translating the sentence into French, one word at a time, left to right, under a causal mask — never allowed to peek ahead. By the time you need to decide whether "it" becomes il (masculine, matching animal) or something else, have you seen the word that resolves it? What would you need that a purely causal model structurally cannot have?

The answer is no — not without cheating. tired, the word that disambiguates it, comes after it in the sentence. A causal decoder reading left to right cannot see it yet. This is exactly why the original Transformer is not one stack but two: an encoder that reads the whole source sentence at once, with no mask at all, and a decoder that generates the target sentence causally — but is allowed to consult the encoder's already-complete understanding whenever it needs to. That consultation is cross-attention, and it is the one mechanism Part 1 didn't cover.

02Two shapes, two jobs

Encoder, decoder, or both — framed by the task

Bidirectional access (encoder) and causal generation (decoder) aren't two versions of the same thing — they're the right tool for structurally different jobs.

ArchitectureAccess patternGood atExample
Encoder-onlyBidirectional — every position sees every otherUnderstanding a complete input: classification, fill-in-the-blankBERT
Decoder-onlyCausal — only backwardGenerating one token at a time from what came beforeGPT, and nearly every current LLM
Encoder-decoderBoth, connected by cross-attentionTransforming one full sequence into a different oneTranslation, summarization — T5, MarianMT

03Assembling the block

One encoder block, narrated

Part 1 §11 compressed an encoder block into one line: x' = x + MHA(LN(x)), x'' = x' + FFN(LN(x')). That's correct and complete — but worth walking as a sequence of decisions rather than a single equation, since "Add & Norm" is doing two separate jobs at once.

This example uses post-norm (LayerNorm after each sub-layer, as the original paper wrote it) vs. the equation above, which followed the now-more-common pre-norm convention (LayerNorm before). Both are used in real models; the residual math is identical either way, only the order of operations shifts.

04The missing mechanism

Cross-attention: the wire between the stacks

Every mechanism Part 1 covered — self-attention, multi-head, causal masking — operates on one sequence talking to itself. Cross-attention is different: it lets the decoder consult the encoder's finished output. Structurally it's the identical softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V from Part 1 §11 — only where Q, K, and V come from changes.

RoleComes from
Query (Q)The decoder's own masked self-attention output — "what am I trying to say next"
Key (K)The encoder's final output — "what the source sentence contains"
Value (V)The encoder's final output — the same tensor K came from

No mask in cross-attention

The decoder's masked self-attention (one step earlier in the same block) enforces "no looking at the future" — but that rule is about the decoder's own sequence. Cross-attention isn't attending within one sequence at all; it's fusing two different, already-complete ones. There is nothing to hide: the full encoder output is legitimately available for every generated token, from the first to the last.

This is not the KV-cache from Part 1 §12

Part 1's KV-cache is an inference-time speedup for the decoder's own self-attention keys and values, which don't change once computed for a given position. The encoder's K and V here are a different tensor entirely — computed once per input sentence (the encoder only runs once), then read by every decoder block's cross-attention layer, every generation step. Same instinct — don't recompute what hasn't changed — different tensor, different mechanism.

05The paper

Why this became the field's reference point

"Attention Is All You Need" wasn't the first encoder-decoder architecture — RNN- and CNN-based sequence-to-sequence models, connected by an earlier form of attention, already existed. What was new was the second sentence of the abstract: dispense with recurrence and convolution entirely. That single choice is what §04's diagram buys you.

RNN-based seq2seqTransformer
Processing orderStrictly sequential — token t waits for token t−1Every position processed in parallel
Long-range infoMust survive being carried step by step; tends to fadeAny two positions are one dot product apart (Part 1 §11)
Training cost~2 months for a comparable task, pre-20173.5 days, 8 GPUs
Cost that grows with lengthLinear in sequence length, but sequentialQuadratic in sequence length (Part 1 §12) — the trade being made

The result reported alongside that speedup was a genuine jump, not a marginal one: 28.4 BLEU on WMT 2014 English→German (modest, a couple of points over prior work) but 41.8 BLEU on English→French — a new single-model state of the art. On an established, hard-to-game benchmark, a large quality jump and a training-time collapse from months to days is the kind of gap a field reorganizes around — the same role ResNet's skip connection played for computer vision.

06From numbers to words

Logits, temperature, and how a decoder actually speaks

The final layer of any decoder — encoder-decoder or decoder-only, it makes no difference here — produces one raw score per vocabulary word: logits, not yet probabilities. Turning them into an actual choice of next word takes a few more decisions, all made after every idea in Parts 1 and 5 has already run.

Predict first — what does dividing logits by a small number do?

Before softmax runs, every logit gets divided by a temperature value. If you divide by something small, like 0.3, does the resulting probability distribution get more confident (peakier) or less? What about dividing by something large, like 3?

p = softmax(logits / T) — small T exaggerates the gaps between logits before softmax sees them, so the distribution sharpens toward the single best guess. Large T compresses the gaps, flattening the distribution toward uniform. This is the same softmax from Part 1 §6, with one extra knob in front of it.

07Reference

Traps and a cheatsheet

Symptom / questionResolution
"Isn't cross-attention just self-attention?"Same formula, different sourcing — K/V from the encoder, Q from the decoder. Self-attention never mixes two different sequences.
"Why no mask in cross-attention?"Masking enforces autoregression within one sequence. Cross-attention fuses two already-complete ones — nothing is "in the future" relative to it.
"Is the encoder's K/V cached like the KV-cache?"Different mechanism. The encoder runs once per input either way; what's reused across decoder steps is its already-finished output, not an inference trick.
Output looks repetitive / stuck in a loopTemperature too low, or top-k/top-p too narrow — the model has no room to pick anything but the single top choice
Output looks incoherentTemperature too high, or top-p threshold too generous — low-probability, low-quality words are staying eligible
"Which should I use, top-k or top-p?"Top-k is simpler and more predictable; top-p adapts its eligible set to the model's confidence, which is usually the better default for open-ended generation
08Summary

What to remember

  • Encoder = bidirectional, understands; decoder = causal, generates; encoder-decoder = both, for transforming one full sequence into another
  • Cross-attention is Part 1's exact attention formula, with K/V sourced from the encoder's finished output and Q sourced from the decoder's own masked self-attention
  • No recurrence was the paper's actual claim — parallel training, not a fundamentally new notion of attention, is what bought the 3.5-day training run
  • Temperature reshapes a probability distribution continuously; top-k and top-p both restrict which words are eligible, by a fixed count or an adaptive probability mass respectively

Nothing in this part is a new formula. It's the same softmax, the same Q/K/V, the same residual connection — pointed at a second sequence, and then at a vocabulary instead of a hidden layer.

09Practice

Exercises

  1. Why can't a decoder-only model translate a full sentence the way an encoder-decoder can, even given unlimited training data?
  2. A decoder block's cross-attention layer and its masked self-attention layer both compute softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V. Name every difference between the two calls.
  3. Temperature is set to 0.01. What happens to generation, and why?
  4. You set top-k = 1. What sampling strategy have you accidentally reproduced?
  5. The encoder for a 20-token sentence runs once. How many times does its output get read by cross-attention over the course of generating a 15-token translation, assuming 6 decoder blocks?
Answers
  1. It can only ever look backward. Resolving something like "it" in Part 1's example sentence requires a word that comes later — structurally invisible to a purely causal model reading left to right, no matter how much data it sees.
  2. Masked self-attention: Q, K, V all come from the decoder's own sequence so far; a causal mask is applied. Cross-attention: Q comes from the decoder, K and V come from the encoder's output; no mask is applied, because there's no "future" within a fused pair of already-complete sequences.
  3. Generation becomes almost perfectly deterministic — the highest-logit word gets pushed to probability ≈1, and the model will very likely produce the exact same output every time given the same input.
  4. Greedy decoding — always take the single highest-probability word, with no randomness at all.
  5. 90 times: 6 decoder blocks × 15 generated tokens, each one running its own cross-attention layer against the same encoder output.
