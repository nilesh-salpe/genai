The maths of neural networks and transformers, built from scratch

Every formula derived from the problem it solves — nothing asserted before it is motivated. Assumes school algebra and a first calculus course. Assumes no machine learning at all. Thirteen of the derivations below are live: change them and watch the argument hold.

Here is where all of it is going. You will not understand this yet — that is the point. By section 12 every piece of it will have been built.

00How to read this

What is assumed, and what is not

Assumed: rearranging equations and function notation; summation notation Σ; exponentials and logarithms (refresher below); basic derivatives and the single-variable chain rule.

Not assumed: any linear algebra course — vectors and matrices are built from scratch here. Any probability course — the two facts needed are derived in Box B. Any programming background.

Every section follows the same shape:

The problem → the picture → building the formula → check it on small numbers

If a formula ever seems to fall from the sky, that is a defect in the explanation. Go back to the picture.

A neural network is a machine with millions of adjustable knobs. Training means working out, for every knob, which way to turn it to make the answer less wrong — then turning them all a tiny bit that way, millions of times.

Box A · refresher
Exponentials and logarithms

These carry more weight here than anything else, so make sure they are solid. An exponential answers "what do I get from multiplying e by itself this many times?" — e² = e·e ≈ 7.39, and the curve fills in every value in between, even fractional exponents (e0.5 ≈ 1.65) and negative ones (e−1 ≈ 0.37). A logarithm asks the reverse question — "e to what power gives me this number?" — so log(7.39) ≈ 2, because e² ≈ 7.39. They undo each other, the same way squaring and square-rooting do.

PropertyWhy it matters here
ex > 0 for every real xThis is what makes softmax possible (§5)

ea·eb = ea+b, so ea/eb = ea−bGives softmax its shift-invariance

Log turns multiplication into addition. That single fact shows up three separate times below.

Box B · refresher
The only two probability facts you need

Skip if you have done a stats course. Otherwise this box unlocks §9 and §11, which are otherwise the two hardest parts of the document.

Variance measures spread: the average squared distance from the mean. Its square root, the standard deviation, is the typical size of a deviation.

Fact 1 — for independent quantities, variances add. Note it is variances that add, not standard deviations. So adding up n independent things each with variance 1 gives variance n, and typical size √n — not n.

Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)

Why √n and not n? Because the terms have random signs and partially cancel.

Fact 2 — for independent quantities with mean zero, variances multiply: Var(XY) = Var(X)·Var(Y). With zero means, Var(XY) = E[X²Y²], and independence splits that into E[X²]E[Y²].

These two facts appear at exactly two places: the √dk scaling (§11) and weight initialization (§9). Both times the reasoning is identical — we are summing n independent products, so the spread grows like √n, so divide by √n to undo it.

01Representing things

Why vectors exist

The problem. Computers only do arithmetic, but we want to work with people, photos, and sentences. So step one is always: turn the thing into numbers.

One number is never enough. A person is a collection of measurements. A photo is millions of brightness values. A word is a few hundred numbers encoding its meaning.

The picture. A vector is a filled-in form — drop the labels, fix the order, and [34, 52000, 8] is a person. There is a second reading that matters later: three numbers are also coordinates, a location in space. So every data point is simultaneously a form and a point. That duality is what lets us ask geometric questions.

02The core operation

The dot product measures agreement

The problem. I have a list of things I care about and a list of what is present. How much do they agree?

A spam filter cares about: the word "free" (+2), sender in contacts (−3), exclamation marks (+1). An email arrives with "free" (1), sender unknown (0), four marks (4):

(2)(1) + (−3)(0) + (1)(4) = 6

Why multiply the pairs rather than add them? Because we want a score that is large only when both are large and pointing the same way. Care a lot but the feature is absent? Contributes nothing. Care negatively and the feature is present? Pulls the score down. Multiplication is the operation encoding "both of these must hold for it to count."

w · x = Σᵢ wᵢxᵢ     two vectors in, one number out

The geometric reading. Define length by Pythagoras, ‖a‖ = √(Σaᵢ²). Then:

a · b = ‖a‖ ‖b‖ cos θ

You can see why in 2D: rotate the axes so a points along x. Then a = (‖a‖, 0) and b = (‖b‖cos θ, ‖b‖sin θ), and the dot product is ‖a‖‖b‖cos θ + 0. Rotating does not change dot products, so this holds generally.

03Doing it in bulk

Matrices, and the shape rule that derives itself

One dot product gives one verdict; I want several. Three questions means three "what I care about" lists, stacked as rows of a grid. That grid is a matrix.

Each row of a weight matrix is one question. Multiplying by the matrix asks every question at once.

(Wx)ᵢ = Σⱼ Wᵢⱼ xⱼ

Matrix multiplication is not a new operation — it is bookkeeping for many dot products at once, which is also why GPUs matter.

The shape rule. People memorise (m×n)(n×p) = (m×p). You do not need to. Every output entry is a dot product between a row of the first and a column of the second — and a dot product needs both lists the same length, so the inner dimensions must match. You get one output per (row, column) pair, so the result is (rows of first) × (columns of second).

Two more operations, needed in §8. Transpose Aᵀ flips the grid across its diagonal. Outer product goes the opposite way from a dot product — two vectors give a whole matrix, a column (2×1) times a row (1×3) giving 2×3. That is the shape of a weight matrix, which is why it appears in the backward pass. Elementwise product ⊙ multiplies matching entries.

The layer, and the bias. A layer is z = Wx + b. Without b, an all-zero input must produce zero and every question's tipping point is stuck at the origin — the same reason school algebra went from y = mx to y = mx + c.

04Depth

Why stacking needs a kink

Stack two linear layers and expand:

W₂(W₁x + b₁) + b₂ = (W₂W₁)x + (W₂b₁ + b₂) = W′x + b′

One layer. Not weaker than two — mathematically identical to one. A thousand layers still collapse. A linear layer can only stretch, rotate and shift space; do that twice and straight lines are still straight. But the boundary between spam and not-spam is not a straight line.

So put a kink between them: ReLU(x) = max(0, x), applied to each entry independently. One bend at the origin is enough — bend enough straight pieces and you trace any curve, and now the algebra above gets stuck at step one.

05Scores into beliefs

Deriving softmax, badly first

The problem. Take any list of real numbers — any sign, any scale — and turn it into a probability distribution: every entry positive, the whole list summing to 1. In a network, that list is the last layer's raw output, called logits: unbounded, possibly negative, meaningless alone. Let us try and fail twice.

softmax(z)ᵢ = ezᵢ ⁄ Σⱼ ezⱼ

Two properties come free. Since ea/eb = ea−b, only the differences between logits matter — so softmax(z + c) = softmax(z). That is not a curiosity: e1000 overflows to infinity, so every real implementation computes softmax(z − max z), which changes nothing mathematically but forces the largest exponent to be e⁰ = 1. This is the log-sum-exp trick. Second, dividing logits by a constant T before softmax controls decisiveness — that is the temperature dial in every LLM API.

Softmax's derivative, and the fact that explains everything downstream

Write S = Σⱼ ezⱼ and sᵢ = ezᵢ/S. Two cases, both by the quotient rule. Same index: zi appears in numerator and sum, giving sᵢ − sᵢ² = sᵢ(1−sᵢ). Different index: zk appears only in the sum, giving −sᵢsk. One line covers both cases using the Kronecker delta δᵢk — shorthand for "1 if i = k, else 0" — which just picks out the same-index case automatically:

∂sᵢ ⁄ ∂zk = sᵢ(δᵢk − sk)
06Beliefs into blame

Deriving cross-entropy from "surprise"

The problem. Assign probability p to an outcome, then watch whether it happens. If it does, how wrong were you to have doubted it? Measure it as surprise: low if you assigned high probability, high if you assigned low. A model that says "70% cat" when the truth is cat should be scored on its surprise at p = 0.70. So what must a surprise function S(p) satisfy?

  1. S(1) = 0. Predicting something with certainty and being right teaches you nothing.
  2. S decreases as p increases. Rarer is more surprising.
  3. Surprises add. Two independent surprises should total. Independent events have probability p·q, so we need S(pq) = S(p) + S(q).

Condition 3 is the sharp one. What function turns multiplication into addition? The logarithm — that is its defining property (Box A).

Write the true label as a one-hot vector y — 1 for the correct class, 0 for every other — and the model's predicted probabilities as ŷ. Summing surprise over classes, only the correct one contributes:

L = −Σk yk log ŷk   which for a one-hot label is just   L = −log ŷcorrect

Why not squared error?

The principled answer. Minimising cross-entropy is exactly maximum likelihood estimation. The probability the model assigns to the whole training set is a product of thousands of small numbers, which underflows — so take logs, turning the product into a sum. Maximising Σ log P(correct) is minimising −Σ log P(correct), which is the cross-entropy. "Train a language model" and "find the parameters making the observed text most likely" are the same sentence.

The practical answer. We just saw the softmax derivative contains s(1−s), which vanishes as s → 0. With squared error that factor survives into the gradient, so a confidently wrong model gets almost no gradient — it learns slowest exactly when it needs to learn most. Cross-entropy's log cancels the factor out. Watch:

The prettiest result in the subject
Deriving ∂L/∂z = ŷ − y

Start from L = −Σᵢ yᵢ log sᵢ and differentiate with respect to logit zk, using d/dx log x = 1/x:

∂L∂zk = −Σᵢ yᵢsᵢ · ∂sᵢ∂zk

Substitute ∂sᵢ/∂zk = sᵢ(δᵢk − sk) — and notice the sᵢ cancels:

= −Σᵢ yᵢ(δᵢk − sk) = −yk + sk Σᵢ yᵢ = sk − yk

since Σᵢ yᵢ = 1. So ∂L/∂z = ŷ − y — predicted minus actual. Said 70% cat and it was a cat? The gradient on that logit is −0.30: push it up, in proportion to how wrong you were. Every exponential and logarithm cancelled. This is why softmax and cross-entropy are permanently married, and why frameworks fuse them into one operation.

Entropy H(p) = −Σ p log p is a distribution's average surprise against itself. KL divergence is the extra surprise from using the wrong distribution. Perplexity = eL: perplexity 20 means the model is as uncertain as if choosing uniformly among 20 options.

07Learning

The chain rule is the whole game

There are millions of knobs. For each we want to know: if I nudge this one up slightly, does the loss rise or fall, and by how much? That is the entire learning problem, and "how does output change when input changes" is precisely a derivative.

Read df/dx = 3 as a sensitivity dial: nudge x by 0.01 and f rises by about 0.03. Collect the partial derivative for every parameter into one vector and you have the gradient — a blame list. Its key property: the gradient points in the direction of steepest increase. (Why: the change in L from a small step d is the dot product ∇L · d, and a dot product is largest when the vectors point the same way.) We want to decrease the loss, so we go the other way.

The problem the chain rule solves. The loss depends on the last layer directly — easy. But the first layer's weights affect the loss only through a long chain. How do we trace responsibility down a chain?

Where the transpose comes from

Take y = Wx + b, with blame δ arriving from above. Forward, the wiring is yᵢ = Σⱼ Wᵢⱼxⱼ: input j connects to output i through a wire of strength Wᵢⱼ.

Blame on the weights. Wᵢⱼ appears in exactly one place, multiplied by xⱼ, so ∂L/∂Wᵢⱼ = δᵢxⱼ — every (i,j) pair gets δᵢ times xⱼ, which is precisely an outer product. In words: a weight's blame is (how wrong its output was) × (how active its input was).

Blame to the input. xⱼ affected every output, so its total blame sums over all of them: ∂L/∂xⱼ = Σᵢ δᵢWᵢⱼ. Look at the indices — we are summing over the first index of W and keeping the second. That is exactly what Wᵀδ computes.

∂L∂W = δ xᵀ     ∂L∂b = δ     ∂L∂x = Wᵀ δ

The transpose is not a trick to memorise. It is what "run the same wiring backwards" looks like in symbols. Forward, W carries signal from inputs to outputs; backward, Wᵀ carries blame back along the identical wires.

Why walk backwards at all? Because there is one loss and millions of parameters. Backwards from the single output gets every gradient in one sweep; forwards would need one sweep per parameter. That asymmetry is the entire reason deep learning is computationally feasible.

Using the blame: gradient descent

Every parameter now has a number attached — its share of the blame, ∂L/∂w. The direction was already decided two paragraphs up: the gradient points toward steepest increase, and we want the loss to go down, so move every parameter a small step the other way.

w ← w − η ∂L/∂w

That is the whole algorithm, applied to every parameter, over and over. η (eta) is the learning rate — how big a step to take. Too small and training crawls; too large and each step overshoots the minimum and the loss climbs instead of falling. The build guide's §6 has a live version of this you can push past that point yourself.

Discouraging large weights: regularization

The problem. Nothing said so far stops a weight from growing arbitrarily large to fit the training data exactly, memorising it rather than learning the underlying pattern. Gradient descent minimises the loss and only the loss — if a huge weight lowers it, gradient descent will happily produce one.

The fix. Add a second term to the loss that charges a price for large weights: L₂ regularization adds λΣw² across every weight in the model, for some small constant λ.

L = Ldata + λ Σ w²

Nothing new is needed to use it — differentiate the new term the same way as everything else. Since d/dw(λw²) = 2λw, it adds exactly one extra term to every weight's gradient:

w ← w − η (∂Ldata/∂w + 2λw)

Read the two pieces separately: the first term still pulls w toward whatever reduces prediction error; the new second term pulls w toward zero, in direct proportion to its own size — a small, constant tax on every weight, every step. This is also called weight decay: with no data gradient at all, this update alone would shrink w geometrically toward zero. λ trades the two pressures against each other — too small and it does nothing, too large and every weight gets taxed to zero regardless of what the data says.

Dropout attacks the same problem differently: during training, randomly zero out a fraction of a layer's activations on every forward pass, chosen fresh each time. A unit cannot come to rely on any one other unit always being present, so the network is pushed toward redundant, robust features instead of fragile, memorised ones. Nothing to differentiate — it changes the forward pass, not the loss.

09What goes wrong

Vanishing gradients, and the three defences

Plain gradient descent has one weakness worth naming before the deeper problem below: it treats every parameter identically, taking the same-size step regardless of how noisy or reliable that parameter's gradient has been. Adam fixes this with two refinements: a running average of the gradient (momentum) and of its square, so each parameter gets its own effective step size; the bias-correction terms 1/(1−βt) exist because both averages start at zero and would otherwise be biased low early on.

The real difficulty is depth. Backprop multiplies a long chain of numbers. If each is slightly under 1 the product shrinks exponentially and early layers never learn. If each is over 1 it explodes to NaN.

Initialization. Too large and activations grow layer over layer; too small and they collapse. Using Box B: a neuron sums nin independent products, variances of independent things add (Fact 1) and variances of independent products multiply (Fact 2), so the output variance is nin × Var(W) × Var(x). Setting Var(W) = 1/nin makes that factor exactly 1. He initialization uses 2/nin, the extra 2 compensating for ReLU zeroing roughly half the inputs.

Normalization. LayerNorm rescales a vector to mean 0 and variance 1, then learns its own scale γ and shift β. The statistics are computed across the features of one vector, not across the batch — which is why transformers use it rather than BatchNorm. BatchNorm's statistics come from the other examples in the batch, making an example's output depend on its neighbours, breaking with variable-length sequences, and unusable when generating one token at a time with batch size 1.

Checkpoint
Everything above is one loop

Sections 1 through 9 built a complete, trainable network — every arrow below is something derived above, and the loop is the entire training algorithm:

input vector→ weighted sum (Wx+b)→ activation→ output→ loss→ gradient→ update↻ back to weighted sum

Represent (§1) → combine (§2–3) → bend (§4) → score and grade (§5–6) → assign blame and nudge downhill (§7–9). From here, one new idea gets added on top of this exact loop: letting each position dynamically choose what to read from the others. That is attention, and it is the whole of what is left.

10Transformers

Words as vectors, and the problem of order

Assigning "cat" = 1, "dog" = 2 is worse than useless — it implies dog is twice cat. Instead give every token a full vector of coordinates, learned by gradient descent like everything else. A sequence of n tokens becomes a matrix with one row per position.

Models work with tokens, not words exactly — chunks produced by splitting text so common words stay whole and rare ones break into pieces. Read "token" as "word-ish chunk"; nothing in the maths changes.

A problem you would not anticipate

The mechanism we are about to build computes, for each token, a weighted sum over all the others. But addition does not care about order: a + b + c = c + b + a. Shuffle the input rows and the outputs shuffle identically, contents unchanged.

"Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would be literally indistinguishable to the architecture. Order is not weakened; it is absent.

Since the machinery sees only the vectors, position must be written into the vectors. What do we need? Every position distinct; values bounded (so appending the raw index is out); and "ten apart" looking similar whether you are at position 20 or 2000.

The picture: clock hands. A clock encodes time in a bounded way using several hands at different speeds — the second hand distinguishes nearby moments, the hour hand distinguishes broad ranges, and every hand stays on the same circle forever. So: many sine waves at wildly different wavelengths, one pair per two coordinates of the embedding.

Below, pos is the token's position, d is the embedding dimension (coordinates per token vector, fixed for the whole model), and i indexes which pair of coordinates — each pair is one "hand," and larger i means a slower hand:

PE(pos, 2i) = sin( pos ⁄ 100002i/d )     PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos( pos ⁄ 100002i/d )
11Attention

Built in five steps, each fixing the last

Take: "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired." What does "it" refer to? To represent "it" properly the model must pull in information from "animal" — six words back. In a different sentence "it" would look somewhere else entirely, so the lookup cannot be hardcoded. Each word must dynamically decide which other words to read from.

Step 1 — score relevance

Token i needs a relevance score for token j. We have the tool: a dot product measures alignment and returns one number. So score(i,j) = xᵢ · xⱼ.

Step 2 — separate "what I seek" from "what I am"

That only scores high when two words are similar. But "it" is not seeking something similar to itself — it is seeking a noun it could refer to. What you want and what you are are different things. So project into two spaces with two learned matrices: Q = XWQ (what I'm looking for) and K = XWK (what I advertise myself as). Now the score is asymmetric, as it should be.

Step 3 — turn scores into weights

Raw scores are unbounded; we want weights summing to 1 so the result is a proper weighted average. §5 already built that: softmax, applied along each row.

Step 4 — decide what gets retrieved

When i attends to j, what does it receive? Not the key — a key is an index label, optimised for being findable, not useful. So a third projection carries the payload: V = XWV. Three roles, three matrices, because "what I want", "how I'm found" and "what I give" are genuinely three different things.

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax( QKᵀ ⁄ √dk ) V

Everything is now motivated except the √dk. This is the most-asked transformer question, so let us derive it.

Step 5 — the scaling factor

Suppose the entries of q and k are roughly independent with mean 0 and variance 1 — which is what initialization and LayerNorm arrange. Their dot product is a sum of dk products. By Box B Fact 2 each product has variance 1; by Fact 1 the variances add:

Var(q · k) = dk   ⟹   typical size ≈ √dk

Causal masking

A model predicting the next token must not let position i see positions after it, or it could simply read off the answer. Before the softmax, set every score where j > i to −∞. Since e−∞ = 0, those weights become exactly zero. (In code, a large negative number like −10⁹.) Each row still sums to 1 — the mask redistributes attention among the permitted tokens rather than shrinking it.

Multi-head, and the block

One set of Q,K,V learns one notion of relevance, but a word needs several at once. So run h attention operations in parallel, each in a subspace of dk = d/h dimensions, then concatenate and mix with WO. Total cost is roughly unchanged — you are partitioning capacity among specialists, not adding it. Output shape equals input shape, which is what lets blocks stack.

x′ = x + MHA(LN(x))     x″ = x′ + FFN(LN(x′))

Attention moves information between positions. The FFN processes each position on its own. Attention gathers; the FFN thinks about what was gathered.

Parameter count per block: attention is four d×d matrices = 4d²; the FFN is d×4d then 4d×d = 8d². So ≈ 12d² per block. At d = 4096 with 32 blocks that is about 6.4 billion parameters, plus embeddings.

12The cost

Attention is quadratic, and everyone cares

Attention computes a score for every pair of positions. n tokens → n² pairs.

That single fact drives an enormous research programme: FlashAttention (identical maths, tiled so the n×n matrix never touches slow memory), sliding-window and sparse attention, linear attention, and state-space models like Mamba.

KV caching. When generating one token at a time, earlier tokens' keys and values never change — so cache them. Each new token then costs O(nd) rather than O(n²d). The cache holds 2 × nlayers × n × d numbers, which becomes the real memory ceiling for long-context serving, and is exactly why Grouped-Query Attention (heads sharing K and V) was invented.

Out the other end. Stack L blocks, apply a final LayerNorm, project to vocabulary size. Softmax gives a probability for every possible next token; cross-entropy against the actual next token gives the loss; backprop gives gradients for all several billion parameters; Adam applies a tiny update. Repeat a few million times. That is a language model, and every piece was built above.

14Practice

Exercises

  1. w = [3, −1, 2], x = [1, 4, 2]. Compute w · x.
  2. W is 5×3 and x has 3 entries. What shape is Wx? How many "questions" is this layer asking?
  3. Show that three stacked linear layers with no activations collapse to one.
  4. Compute softmax([0, 1, 1]).
  5. Explain why softmax([100, 101]) and softmax([0, 1]) give the same answer.
  6. A model assigns 0.2 to the correct class. What is the cross-entropy loss? What if it assigns 0.8?
  7. In §8, the gradient for row 1 of W₁ was all zeros. Why — and what would fix it?
  8. A layer has x = [2, 3] and receives blame δ = [0.5, −1]. Compute ∂L/∂W.
  9. If dk = 256, what is the typical size of an unscaled q · k? Why is that a problem?
  10. Context grows from 2,000 to 32,000 tokens. By what factor does attention's cost grow?
Answers
  1. 3(1) + (−1)(4) + 2(2) = 3 − 4 + 4 = 3.
  2. (5×3)(3×1) = 5×1. Five outputs, so five questions.
  3. W₃(W₂(W₁x + b₁) + b₂) + b₃ = (W₃W₂W₁)x + (W₃W₂b₁ + W₃b₂ + b₃) = W′x + b′.
  4. e⁰ = 1, e¹ = 2.718 twice; sum = 6.437. Result [0.155, 0.422, 0.422] — equal logits get equal probability, as they must.
  5. Softmax is shift-invariant: only differences matter, because ea/eb = ea−b. Both pairs differ by 1.
  6. −ln(0.2) = 1.61. −ln(0.8) = 0.22. Four times more confident cut the loss about sevenfold.
  7. Hidden unit 0 had z = −0.5 < 0, so ReLU output zero and its derivative was zero, blocking all blame. Blame = error × activity, and the activity was zero. Fixes: leaky ReLU, a smooth activation like GELU, better initialization, or a lower learning rate so units are not pushed into the dead zone.
  8. Outer product δxᵀ = [[1.0, 1.5], [−2.0, −3.0]].
  9. About √256 = 16, so scores span roughly ±16. Softmax of gaps that large is effectively one-hot, and a saturated softmax has derivative s(1−s) ≈ 0 — attention receives almost no gradient. Hence dividing by √dk.
  10. Length grew 16×, and attention is O(n²), so cost grows about 256×.

One page

A neural network is a machine with millions of knobs:

  • Represent things as vectors — lists of numbers that are also points in space
  • Combine them with weighted questions — matrix multiplication, many dot products at once
  • Bend the result so depth means something — nonlinear activations
  • Convert scores to beliefs (softmax) and beliefs to a wrongness score (cross-entropy = surprise)
  • Assign blame to every knob — the chain rule, run backwards
  • Nudge every knob downhill — gradient descent, a few million times

A transformer adds exactly one new idea:

Let every position dynamically decide which other positions to read from — score relevance with a dot product, normalise with softmax, retrieve a weighted blend.

That is attention. Every tool it uses was already on the list. If you can derive softmax, explain why the loss is −log p, run the chain rule backwards through a linear layer, and give the variance argument for √dk, you understand the mathematics of modern AI.

Part two of this course builds a working digit classifier from these formulas — every shape, every parameter, and sixty lines of NumPy with a hand-written backward pass.
